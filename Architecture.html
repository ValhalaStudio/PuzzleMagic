What this is

A block-placement puzzle in the Woodoku family, built with Unreal Engine 5.8 and C++. It targets iPhone (portrait, touch first) and runs on Windows, where it also gets a ray-traced cathedral.

You drag pieces from a tray of three onto a 9×9 board. Any full row, column or 3×3 box clears. The rules live in one plain object, UPuzzleManager, which changes state the moment a piece lands and broadcasts events. What you see and hear follows later, timed to the animations: the game mode turns rule events into sound, haptics, camera shake and UI, and the cathedral reads your luck to decide how dark the room gets.

Board

9×9 cells and 27 lines that can clear: 9 rows, 9 columns, 9 boxes. A tray of 3 pieces plus HOLD, drawn from 25 shapes.

Combo

A clear starts a combo that survives 3 placements without a clear. Multi-line clears climb faster; a line of one sigil is Blessed; the box inside the rune circle scores ×3.

Moves

Each piece costs a move; clears refund moves. Endless starts with 30. Quest levels carry their own budget and earn 1 to 3 stars from the moves left.

Relics

One every 3 combo steps, at most 3 of each. Holy Light purges a 3×3 area; Reroll replaces the tray.

Curses

Gargoyle stones land every few moves (every 8 in Endless): a clear cracks one, a second shatters it. Omens strike too: lightning turns a tile to stone, a hex takes 2 moves.

Luck

Starts at 20. +6 per combo step, −20 for Holy Light, −12 for Reroll, −10 per ward. It is the chance, up to 90%, that an omen is warded off.

Modes

Endless (best score saved) and 15 quest levels, plus a demo mode where a greedy bot plays.

Presentation

Ray-traced cathedral on PC, a dread layer that wakes as luck runs out, an interface built in C++, a synthesized Gregorian score and a MetaSound ambience.

Architecture

Input reaches the rules, the rules change the board and announce what happened, and the game mode decides how it looks and sounds.

Decisions that shape the code

  1. Rules never touch visuals. UPuzzleManager is a UObject with no rendering. It updates the board state immediately and broadcasts events; the board plays animations with delays, and the game mode times sound, haptics and UI to them.
  2. The UI is code. UPuzzleHUDWidget builds every widget in C++ from two materials (enamel panel, embossed icon) and two fonts. Nothing lives in a Widget Blueprint, so the UI diffs and reviews like the rest.
  3. Every asset comes from a script. Materials are HLSL custom nodes written by Python in the editor, sounds are synthesized, meshes are procedural or scripted in Blender, and textures come from ComfyUI.
  4. Rebuildable assets load at run time. An asset held by a C++ constructor (ConstructorHelpers) is rooted inside the editor commandlet, and rebuilding it there crashes. Newer assets use LoadObject so their scripts can rebuild them in place.
  5. All tiles share one mesh. MeshBuffers builds the tile once as a realtime mesh and every tile's component points at it: one set of GPU buffers for the whole board.
  6. PC gets ray tracing; iPhone gets the mobile renderer. Lumen and MegaLights don't run on iOS in 5.8, so the phone look has to be authored for the mobile renderer.
  7. Visual changes are checked in screenshots. Logs stayed clean while the camera faced the sky and while meshes rendered inside out, so every visual change is verified with a capture of the running game.

A move, end to end

What happens between dropping a piece and the last popup fading. Times come from AGridManager::ArriveDuration (0.5 s) and UPuzzleManager::OmenDelay (1.1 s) in the code.

Start-up, in order

  1. APuzzleGameMode::StartPlay loads or creates the save (UPuzzleSaveGame::LoadOrCreate) and creates the rules.
  2. It spawns AGridManager, APuzzleInputHandler and AGothicEnvironment, binds the rules to the board and gives the input handler both.
  3. It binds the seven rule events, creates the demo bot and frames the camera on the board and tray.
  4. It starts the music playlist: the chant twice, then the organ piece, crossfaded.
  5. It reads the launch flags (see Run & tune) and opens the menu, with How to Play on the very first launch, or goes straight to a demo or a level.

Meanwhile AGothicEnvironment::BeginPlay builds the wall, stained glass, piers, flagstones, mist and candles, attaches the convolution reverb to the SM_Cathedral submix and builds the dread layer: tentacles, eyes, fog volumes, motes, the madness post-process and the MetaSound ambience.

Why 9×9, not 8×8

The first request was an 8×8 board. The rules decided it: they clear rows, columns and 3×3 boxes, and boxes only tile a board whose side is a multiple of three.

On 8×8 the last row and column of boxes would be 3×2, 2×3 and 2×2 fragments. They are smaller targets, easier to fill than a real box, so the best play would drift to the board's right and bottom edges. Dropping boxes altogether would leave 16 lines and turn the game into a plain row-and-column puzzle.

On 9×9 the board splits into nine whole boxes, like a Sudoku grid, and 27 lines can clear instead of 16. Several features depend on whole boxes:

  • The blessed box. One of the nine is marked with a rune circle, and clearing it scores ×3.
  • Holy Light purges a 3×3 area around a chosen cell, with its centre clamped to cells 1 to 7 so it always stays on the board.
  • Box shading. Slots alternate indigo and burgundy per box (AGridManager::BuildBoardVisuals), so each box reads at a glance.
  • Goals and the bot. Quest levels ask you to clear boxes, and the demo bot aims Holy Light at the densest 3×3 area (FindDensestArea).

It also fits the phone. 9 cells at 90 units each make an 810-unit board inside the 1080-unit portrait canvas, with room for the tray below and the score above.
